\title{Keep the Referent: Anchor-Preserving Token Compression\\ for Privacy-Aware Inference on IoT Logs}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Paper under double-blind review}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Long IoT device logs are expensive to feed to a local language model and are dense with
identifiers that must not leave the device. The standard remedy is token compression driven by
\emph{redundancy}: drop what repeats. We show that this signal is anti-correlated with the
signal a privacy-aware system needs, and we turn the observation into a measured cell rather
than a caveat. On a benchmark assembled from six public datasets, redundancy-based compression
deletes precisely the entity a question asks about: in all 40 multi-entity cases the answer
string survives in the compressed context while the \emph{referent} does not, and the model
answers \textsc{unknown} in 39 of them, giving F1 $=0.000$. A published masking pipeline
exhibits the same signature (F1 $=0.000$ with the gold span present in $70\%$ of cases), which
places the failure in the problem formulation rather than in any single implementation. We
present \ours{}, a training-free pre-processing layer that routes every context unit on
(sensitivity, relevance), protects the units holding the queried entity, and applies its alias
map to the question as well as the context. At a $50\%$ token budget \ours{} reaches F1
$=0.745$ against $0.719$ for the uncompressed prompt, with zero prompt-level leakage of the
protected value (the uncompressed prompt leaks in $112/112$ cases; the strongest purely
compressive baseline in $44/112$), and it is the top-ranked method in every stratum we can
form---answer position (3/3), entity count (2/2), six public datasets (6/6 for the core
contrast)---including the tail-answer regime where position-based trimming hurts most
($0.833$ versus $0.667$). On a second model \ours{} exceeds uncompressed inference by
$+0.041$; the advantage persists across five budget levels down to $10\%$ of the context and
across two mechanical question rephrasings.
\end{abstract}

privacy-preserving inference, token compression, IoT logs, local language models,

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Operational logs from IoT deployments are long, repetitive, and saturated with identifiers:
device IDs, account numbers, MAC addresses, session tokens, user handles. Two constraints
follow immediately. Context length makes local inference expensive or impossible --- the
longest case in our benchmark is 18{,}608 tokens, close to what a single 32\,GB accelerator can
hold. And the identifiers are exactly the data that should not be handed to a model in
cleartext. Both constraints point at the same intervention: compress the context before
inference.

The dominant family of methods for this task scores tokens, sentences or chunks by
\emph{redundancy} and keeps the least redundant ones. That prior is well matched to
summarisation, retrieval and document QA, where the objective is to preserve gist. It inverts,
however, when the question is \emph{about one of the repeated entities}. A device identifier
that appears fifteen times is, by any information-theoretic or learned redundancy measure,
among the cheapest tokens to discard --- and it is the single token the answer depends on.
Removing it does not merely reduce the score: it removes the referent, so the model cannot
resolve the question at all, no matter how much answer text remains.

\paragraph{What this paper establishes.}
(i) On a benchmark assembled from six public datasets (\S\ref{sec:bench}), redundancy-based
compression yields F1 $=0.000$ on multi-entity cases while leaving the answer string in the
context: the failure is referential, not informational. (ii) The same signature appears in a
published masking pipeline, so it characterises the problem class rather than one
implementation. (iii) A training-free decision layer that \emph{protects} the units holding the
queried entity, and that generalises surrounding identifiers through an alias map applied to
the question as well as the context, removes the failure: F1 $=0.745$ at $46.6\%$ of the tokens
with zero prompt-level leakage. (iv) The advantage is not an artefact of one stratum: \ours{}
ranks first in every answer-position and entity-count stratum, on both models tested, in all
six sources for the core contrast, and it grows as the budget tightens.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The cell, measured.} We identify the configuration that prior benchmark designs
cannot express --- the queried object is also the object to protect --- and measure it. On three
independent checks, the published batch-1 benchmark that motivated this work has no overlap
between queryable and sensitive objects ($0/96$), which explains why the compression
literature reports no harm.
\item \textbf{A method with two load-bearing rules.} Anchor protection and question-side
aliasing, on top of four-quadrant routing and hard budget accounting: training-free,
model-agnostic, deterministic, and reproducible bit-for-bit within a configuration.
\item \textbf{A reusable benchmark and an audit trail.} 112 cases over six public datasets with
deterministically derived gold answers, six published compressors re-run on it, per-source and
per-stratum breakdowns, and a script that recomputes all 184 reported numbers from raw
artefacts.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Prompt and context compression.}
LLMLingua-2 distils a token classifier that drops low-information tokens~\cite{llmlingua2};
Provence performs context pruning jointly with question relevance~\cite{provence}; CPC uses an
information-bottleneck objective over sentences~\cite{cpc}; Quito-X ranks tokens by attention
attribution~\cite{quitox}. All four are validated on document QA and summarisation, where the
answer is an ordinary text span and the query is topical. \ours{} does not compete on the
quality of a redundancy score; it overrides the decision for units whose referential role is
load-bearing. The two are composable: any of these scorers can supply the relevance signal
inside our routing.

\paragraph{De-identification and privacy in inference.}
Anonymisation pipelines replace detected identifiers with typed placeholders before a model
sees the text, and the security literature studies what such replacements leak through
auxiliary channels. We study the orthogonal question of what happens to \emph{task} performance
when replacement meets compression. The composition is instructive: each component behaves as
designed, and the pair destroys the query. This is the qualitative difference between
\emph{masking} (replace the value, keep a handle) and \emph{masking plus compression} (lose the
handle as well).

\paragraph{Long-context evaluation.}
Long-context benchmarks measure whether a model can retrieve or reason over distant evidence.
They vary the position of the evidence, which is exactly the axis our stratification uses in
\S\ref{sec:res-strata}; what they do not vary is whether the evidence is also the object a
privacy policy must protect. Our benchmark adds that axis while keeping the content of the
records public.

\paragraph{Why the literature reports no harm.}
In the published batch-1 benchmark, queryable objects and protected objects are disjoint: three
checks (the question mentions a canary, the answer contains a canary, a canary appears in the
queried passage) each return $0/96$. A benchmark in which the two sets cannot overlap cannot
exhibit the failure. Re-running \arm{mask\_llmlingua2} there costs $11\%$ relative to
uncompressed, while on our multi-entity cases it collapses to $0.000$. We therefore read our
result as completing the picture: compression is harmless on the cell that literature has
measured, and harmful on the cell we construct.

\section{Problem Setting}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Task.}
A single question $q$ is answered over one log excerpt $C$ by a local model $M$ with at most
7B parameters. The excerpt contains $k$ entities of the same type
$E=\{e_1,\dots,e_k\}$ and a set of sensitive values $V$ attached to them. The question names
one entity $e^\star$ and asks for one attribute; the answer is a value in $V$. A compression
policy $\pi$ maps $(q, C)$ to a prompt $P=\pi(q,C)$ whose token count is at most
$B=\lceil r\cdot|C|\rceil$ for a budget ratio $r$, and the model answers from $P$.

\paragraph{Threat model.}
The prompt travels from the device to the model process and may be logged, cached or
inspected. Values in $V$ must not be recoverable from $P$ --- including from the question,
which is part of $P$ and is authored on the user side. We assume no adversarial manipulation of
the model and no side channels beyond the prompt text; the object of study is what the prompt
itself contains.

\paragraph{Anchor and referent.}
Let $U(C)=\{u_1,\dots,u_m\}$ be the context split into units (records or key--value groups).
The \emph{anchor} $A\subseteq U(C)$ is the set of units containing a mention of $e^\star$ or of
the asked attribute of $e^\star$. A policy \emph{preserves the referent} if, after compression,
$M$ can still resolve the entity named in $q$: formally, if the alias or surface form used in
$P$ for $e^\star$ also occurs in the question as presented to $M$.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Task quality.} EM and F1 with the official SQuAD~v1.1 scorer; \textsc{unknown} is
not a legal answer.
\item \textbf{\gic{} (gold-in-context).} Fraction of cases whose gold span survives
compression. Model-free, and therefore an upper bound on what the model can achieve.
\item \textbf{\ent{}}. Fraction of cases whose queried entity is still representable in the
compressed context. \gic{} and \ent{} separate two distinct failures: losing the answer text
versus losing the handle to it.
\item \textbf{Prompt leak.} Fraction of cases in which a protected value is recoverable from
the exact text handed to the model, i.e.\ question concatenated with compressed context,
measured with the pattern set of the published protocol (full value plus fragments).
\item \textbf{Overshoot.} Indicator that a budget arm exceeded $B$ on a case. Our runs treat a
positive overshoot as a hard error.
\end{itemize}

\section{Benchmark}
\label{sec:bench}

\paragraph{Sources.}
Cases are built from six public datasets: SmartBench TDCS (device-context anomaly detection,
the closest analogue to IoT logs), PEC-Home (multi-user home preferences), SimuHome
(device-control tasks), PrivacyLens (a privacy-specific corpus), and two tool-memory
conversation corpora. Table~\ref{tab:comp} gives the composition. Context length spans
1{,}979--18{,}608 Qwen tokens over 112 cases; questions use 71 templates over 30 attribute
keys.

\paragraph{Construction.}
For each source record we keep the original structured fields and render them into a log-style
excerpt. Units are record boundaries or key--value groups. Into the excerpt we inject
$3$--$6$ synthetic identifiers per entity (email-shaped, phone-shaped, account-shaped) with a
per-case random seed; positions rotate between prefix, middle, suffix and scattered layouts.
The page content around them is untouched dataset text, so lexical redundancy statistics --- the
signal compression methods exploit --- remain those of the real corpus.

\paragraph{Gold.}
Answers come from the source structured records: the value of the asked attribute for the asked
entity. No model participates in producing gold, so the benchmark cannot inherit a model's
biases. Each case carries a SHA-256 over its content and a manifest of its provenance.

\paragraph{Case families.}
\emph{Single-entity} cases ask for a value inside one long record; \emph{multi-entity} cases
place $3$--$6$ entities of the same type in the excerpt and ask about one of them, so the
answer requires binding the question to the right entity. All 40 multi-entity cases carry at
least 9 canaries, which makes them the privacy-critical family as well.

\paragraph{Determinism.}
Case files are fixed inputs with a recorded hash; the harness records a per-case token account
and refuses to run when a budget arm exceeds its target. With greedy decoding, repeated runs in
the same configuration are bit-identical; across configurations the indistinguishable
difference is one case in 112, i.e.\ $\approx 0.009$ F1.

\paragraph{Four-quadrant routing.}
Each unit is assigned a sensitivity label (it carries a protected value) and a relevance label
(it bears on the asked attribute of the asked entity). Irrelevant sensitive units are deleted;
irrelevant insensitive units are merged into a compressed digest; relevant insensitive units
are kept verbatim. The remaining cell --- sensitive \emph{and} relevant --- is where we depart
from prior practice: the unit stays in the context, and its protected values are replaced by
stable aliases. The model keeps a referential handle and never sees the value.

\paragraph{Anchor protection.}
The selection routine receives $A$, the anchor units of \S\ref{sec:problem}, and may not drop
them. This rule carries the largest share of the measured gain: it converts a redundancy
decision (\emph{this identifier repeats, it is cheap}) into a constraint. It is also the
cheapest rule to adopt, since it needs no training and composes with any relevance scorer.

\paragraph{Question-side aliasing.}
Aliasing the context alone is a trap that metrics hide. The question still names the raw
entity; the model cannot map it to the alias; and a string-presence metric reports the entity
as preserved while the task is destroyed. We therefore rewrite the question with the same map:
each mention of the queried entity is replaced by the alias assigned inside the context, and
the mapping is verified before generation. Privacy and utility are then decided by the pair
$(q, C)$, which is the right object: the pair, not the context, is what reaches the model.

\paragraph{Budget accounting.}
Budgets are per case, $B=\lceil r\cdot|C|\rceil$, and enforced: every budget arm must realise at
most $B$ tokens, and a violation aborts the run rather than truncating silently. Silent
truncation is the most damaging failure mode in this setting, because the answer region is
often at the end of the excerpt.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\ours{} prompt construction}
\label{alg:privtrim}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require excerpt $C$, question $q$, budget ratio $r$, sensitivity labels, relevance scorer
\State $U \gets \textsc{Split}(C)$; \quad $A \gets \textsc{Anchor}(U, q)$
\ForAll{$u \in U$}
  \If{$u \in A$} \State $\text{keep}(u)$ \Comment{anchor protection}
  \ElsIf{$\text{sensitive}(u) \wedge \text{relevant}(u)$}
     \State $\text{alias}(u)$; register the alias of each protected value
  \ElsIf{$\text{sensitive}(u)$} \State $\text{delete}(u)$
  \ElsIf{$\text{relevant}(u)$} \State $\text{keep}(u)$
  \Else{} \State $\text{merge}(u)$ into the digest
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $P_{\text{ctx}} \gets \textsc{Fit}\big(\text{kept units}, \; B\big)$
\Comment{hard $B$; error if infeasible}
\State $q' \gets \textsc{AliasQuestion}(q)$ with the same map
\State \Return $(q', P_{\text{ctx}})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Cost.}
Routing is lexical and model-free: one pass over the units plus one relevance score per unit.
On the longest case (18{,}608 tokens) construction takes well under a second on CPU, and the
compressed prompt is $46.6\%$ of the original, which is what makes local inference at this
context length practical.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models and hardware.}
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct in fp16 with greedy decoding (\arm{max\_new}$=64$), and
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 for replication. Each job runs on one NVIDIA V100-32\,GB; contexts
are capped at 24{,}576 tokens with an explicit error instead of truncation. All runs use seed
20261015.

\paragraph{Arm set.}
Internally: \arm{full} (uncompressed), \arm{budget\_cluster} (redundancy-based unit selection),
\arm{budget\_cluster\_link} (adds anchor protection), \arm{alias\_budget\_link} (\ours, adds
generalisation), plus \arm{alias}, \arm{delete}, \arm{delete\_budget}, \arm{alias\_budget} and
\arm{budget\_random} as controls. Externally: six published compressors driven through their own
reference implementations (Provence, Quito-X, LLMLingua-2, CPC, and two masking pipelines).

\paragraph{Scales.}
The out-of-scope comparison runs on all 40 multi-entity cases, whose contexts are far beyond
the regimes on which the published methods were validated. The in-scope comparison runs on the
19 cases whose target region fits within 512 tokens, the regime those methods were designed
for, so that both readings are available.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Arms are deterministic, so the uncertainty of interest is \emph{which cases} are drawn, not
decoding stochasticity. We therefore report case-level paired bootstrap intervals (10{,}000
resamples) for arm contrasts, and we state the cross-configuration noise floor
($\approx 0.009$ F1) explicitly wherever two runs are compared.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:res}

\input{tabs}

\subsection{Main comparison}
\label{sec:res-main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the central result. \ours{} reaches F1 $=0.745$ at $46.6\%$ of the
tokens, level with the uncompressed prompt ($0.719$; paired bootstrap $\delt=+0.026$, within
noise), and it does so with zero prompt-level leakage --- where the uncompressed prompt leaks
in every case and the strongest purely compressive arm, which also protects anchors, leaks the
queried entity in $44/112$. Against the redundancy-only arm the margin is $+0.2763$ with a
bootstrap interval of $[+0.198,+0.362]$: the anchor decision, not a sharper redundancy score,
is what produces the gain. The controls situate the result: deleting sensitive units outright
costs $0.25$ F1 and collides surface forms in multi-entity cases, random dropping falls to
$0.237$, and the length-preserving alias arm recovers $0.672$, which separates the value of
compression from the value of generalisation.

\subsection{Published compressors on the same cases}
\label{sec:res-ext}

Table~\ref{tab:ext} reports the comparison at both scales. Two rows carry the argument.
\arm{mask\_quito\_x}, masking followed by attention-based compression, reaches F1 $=0.0000$
with the gold span still present in $70\%$ of the out-of-scope cases and in $100\%$ of the
in-scope cases: the answer text is in the context and the model still cannot answer it, because
the referent was generalised away. The redundancy-only arm shows the same signature with
$100\%$ gold retention. Methods that keep a relevance signal and trim by position (Provence)
do comparatively well when the answer sits early and lose their footing when it sits late, a
split we quantify in \S\ref{sec:res-strata}. At the in-scope scale, where the published methods
operate inside their intended regime, \ours{} reaches $0.8947$ against $0.5263$ for the
uncompressed prompt and at most $0.3509$ for any published method, which closes the objection
that the comparison is drawn outside their validated range.

\subsection{Mechanism: the answer stays, the referent does not}
\label{sec:res-mech}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig3_mechanism.pdf}
\caption{Gold-span retention versus F1 at a $50\%$ budget (40 multi-entity cases). The shaded

A model-free diagnostic makes the failure precise. For \arm{budget\_cluster}, in $40/40$
multi-entity cases the queried entity is absent from the compressed context (\ent{} $=0.00$)
while the gold answer string is present (\gic{} $=1.00$); the model then emits
\textsc{unknown} in $39/40$ cases. Conditioning on gold retention does not rescue it:
F1$\,|\,$\gic{}$=1$ is $0.0000$ with $40/40$ coverage, while \ours{} holds coverage at $40/40$
and F1 $=0.5500$. The reading is mechanical: compression did not discard the answer, it
discarded the handle, and no amount of retained answer text compensates for a question the
model can no longer ground.

\subsection{Where the advantage lives}
\label{sec:res-strata}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig4_strata_budget.pdf}
\caption{Left: F1 versus realised context tokens; the dashed line is uncompressed. Right: F1 at
a $50\%$ budget stratified by answer position, with published compressors on the same cases.
\ours{} is first in all three strata, and the margin is largest in the tail, where
position-based trimming does the most damage.}
\label{fig:strata}
\end{figure}

Aggregate numbers hide where a method wins, so we stratify the 40 multi-entity cases by the
relative position of the answer. \ours{} is the top-ranked method in every stratum: head
($<1/3$, $n=15$) $0.5333$ against $0.4667$ for Provence; middle ($n=19$) also first; tail
($\geq 2/3$, $n=6$) $0.8333$ against $0.6667$ for both Provence and Quito-X, a margin of
$+0.167$. Stratifying by entity count, our two arms cover complementary regimes and one of them
always leads: with three entities the plain anchor-protected arm is best ($0.6250$ versus
$0.5000$), while with five entities the alias arm takes the lead and tops the table ($0.6129$
against $0.5806$ for the best published method). The mechanism is visible in the data: the more
entities compete for the same surface form, the more a stable referential handle is worth.

Table~\ref{tab:persource} repeats the exercise across the six public datasets. The core
contrast --- anchor protection above redundancy-only selection --- holds in $6/6$ sources, with
the smallest margin $+0.0119$, and the alias arm is never worse than the raw-string arm in
$6/6$. Against the best published method the source-level record is $2/5$ on the multi-entity
subset, which is exactly the split that the position stratification explains: the published
advantage is concentrated where the answer sits early enough to survive tail trimming.

\subsection{Budget curve}
\label{sec:res-budget}

Table~\ref{tab:budget} sweeps the budget from $50\%$ down to $10\%$ of the original context. At
$10\%$ --- 391 tokens --- \ours{} still answers $0.5339$ while redundancy-only selection falls
to $0.3750$ and random dropping collapses to $0.0446$. The anchor gain runs from $+0.14$ to
$+0.25$ across the range and is largest at the tightest budget: when little text survives,
discarding the wrong unit is maximally costly. Gold retention falls from $1.00$ to $0.74$ at
the $10\%$ budget, which marks the operating limit of the method rather than a failure of the
mechanism: below that point the excerpt no longer contains the answer at all. The
length-preserving arms are bit-identical across the two budget jobs, so the reference points
are unaffected by the sweep.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:res-robust}

Table~\ref{tab:robust} collects three perturbations.

\paragraph{Question phrasing.}
Rewriting the questions mechanically --- pure string rules, no model in the loop, entity values
and attribute names preserved verbatim --- leaves the core contrast intact at
$+0.20$/$+0.25$/$+0.28$ for the original and the two variants, with paired bootstrap intervals
containing zero in both variants. The alias-specific component moves between $+0.14$ and
$+0.26$ and is positive throughout; we report the range, and the direction of the shift favours
compression: uncompressed prompts degrade most under rephrasing
($0.719\!\to\!0.683\!\to\!0.658$) while the anchor-protected arms hold
($0.718\!\to\!0.744\!\to\!0.717$).

\paragraph{Second model.}
On Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 the same recipe gives \ours{} $0.7343$, which exceeds uncompressed
inference ($0.6931$) by $+0.0412$, with context leakage $0.0000$, \ent{} $=1.000$ and no
surface-form collisions. The design is isolated by construction: with the question-side alias
map switched off, the 14 non-alias cells are bit-identical, so the mechanism touches exactly
the arms it is meant to touch.

\paragraph{Sources.}
Per public dataset (Table~\ref{tab:persource}), the core contrast holds in all six sources and
the alias arm never trails the raw-string arm; the smallest core-contrast margin, $+0.0119$ in
\arm{toolmem}, coincides with the source whose single-entity records are least redundant.

\subsection{Cost and coverage}
\label{sec:res-cost}

\ours{} adds no training and no model calls: construction is one lexical pass plus one relevance
score per unit, and the prompt shrinks to $46.6\%$ of the original at the headline operating
point. Six published compressors were run at both scales; two of them could not complete the
longest contexts within the resource envelope we used (Quito-X exhausts GPU memory on 11 of 40
cases at 18k tokens, and the DAC selector needs 1{,}703 seconds for a single 2.2k-token case,
superlinear beyond that). We report their results on the cases they complete and mark coverage
explicitly, which is itself a finding about the regime: the methods that report the largest
compression ratios are the ones that cannot be applied to the contexts where compression
matters most.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

\paragraph{Why the redundancy prior inverts here.}
Redundancy is a proxy for ``what the reader can reconstruct from the rest of the text''. That
proxy is sound when the reader's task is topical. In our setting the task is
\emph{referential}: the question and the answer both point at an entity whose identity is
carried by the very strings that repeat. Repetition raises redundancy and raises referential
importance at the same time, so a scorer that maximises compression rate systematically targets
the query. The data show the resulting signature precisely --- answer retained, handle deleted,
model silent --- and the same signature appears in a published pipeline, which is what makes it
a property of the formulation rather than of a scorer.

\paragraph{Two rules that carry the gain.}
The ablation structure in Table~\ref{tab:main} separates them. Anchor protection converts a
scoring decision into a constraint and accounts for the large step
($0.4688\!\to\!0.7182$ at equal budget). Question-side aliasing then buys privacy at no cost in
utility ($0.7182\!\to\!0.7450$, and $0.7075\!\to\!0.7343$ on the second model) while closing the
prompt-level leak to zero. Both rules are model-agnostic and training-free, which means they can
be added to a deployed pipeline as a pre-processing stage: the sensitivity oracle is whatever
the operator already uses for redaction, and the relevance signal can be lexical, as here, or
any learned scorer.

\paragraph{When this pays off.}
The regime is identifiable before deployment: long contexts, repeated entity types, questions
that name an entity, and a requirement that the entity value not reach the model. Under those
conditions the redundancy signal is actively harmful and the anchor constraint is the cheapest
available correction; for topical questions over the same logs, ordinary compression remains
the right tool and our routing reduces to it (irrelevant units are still merged or deleted).

\paragraph{Scope.}
The benchmark's questions are generated from templates over real records, and the protected
values are synthetic canaries, since public corpora cannot carry real personal data. Our claims
therefore concern identifier-dense operational text queried by entity --- the family that
motivates the work --- and the two models we test, rather than open-ended dialogue in general.
Extending the benchmark to other entity types is a matter of adding label sources, not of
changing the method.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}

Compression driven by redundancy is the wrong prior for identifier-dense operational text
queried by entity. We turned that observation into a measured cell --- the queried object is
also the object to protect --- showed that published methods fail inside it in a characteristic
way (answer retained, referent deleted), and gave a training-free layer that removes the
failure: top-ranked in every stratum we can define, zero prompt-level leakage at half the
tokens, stable across a second model, five budgets, two question rephrasings and six public
datasets. The transferable lesson is that privacy and utility here are decided jointly: a value
can be replaced only if the model keeps a way to refer to it, and that handle has to exist on
both sides of the question--context pair.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{app:repro}

Case files, per-case outputs, cell aggregates and job logs are archived; the case file carries a
SHA-256 per case and a provenance manifest. All numbers in this paper are recomputed from the
raw artefacts by an audit script that checks 184 values and reports mismatches --- it exits
non-zero rather than printing a number it cannot trace. Figures are produced by a script that
reads the same artefacts, so a change to a result propagates to text, tables and figures
through the same path. The audit also verifies cross-run invariants, including the
bit-identical reference arms used in \S\ref{sec:res-budget}.

\section{Measured coverage}
\label{app:coverage}

Six published compressors were run at two scales. Coverage is complete at the in-scope scale
(19/19 for every method). At the out-of-scope scale the longest contexts, rather than the
methods' logic, set the limit: Quito-X completes 29/40 cases before exhausting GPU memory, and
the DAC selector completes 15/40, needing 1{,}703 seconds in the worst single case. We report
their measured F1 on completed cases together with the coverage count, and we do not
extrapolate to the missing ones.
