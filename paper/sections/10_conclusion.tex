\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied what happens to a privacy-preserving inference pipeline when the entity a
question asks about is also the entity that must be protected. Both standard remedies fail
there, for opposite reasons: redaction removes the referent the reader needs, and redundancy
ordering removes the record that carries the answer at close to chance, because the score
ranks units by self-similarity and knows nothing about which record the question is about.
\textsc{DARK} resolves the conflict by deciding the two axes per unit and giving the
sensitive-and-relevant cell the one operation neither remedy provides: the unit stays, its
values are aliased, and the same map is written into the question. Relevance is read from the
question---the record block that mentions the queried entity---so the pipeline needs neither a
reference answer nor training.

Across a 112-case benchmark over six public datasets, \textsc{DARK} keeps $48.6\%$ of the
tokens at zero prompt-level leakage, at the same F1 as aliasing the full context without
compression. On a $2{,}728$-case extension the leakage result is the stronger one: $0\%$ of
protected values remain recoverable where the strongest query-aware compressor leaves $98.1\%$,
and F1 is level with that compressor, since the interval on the difference contains zero. Against
the strongest leak-free published baseline it is ahead by $0.3226$ F1 points. The 2026 rule-based
compressor we re-run needs no model at all, yet costs more tokens than we spend and still leaks
in every case, because its rules delete the record syntax the question depends on. Replacing the
alias operation with deletion is what moves those numbers: it costs $0.1795$ F1 overall, $0.3273$
on multi-entity cases, and nothing where no anchor exists, so the rule acts on the structure it
was designed for.

Crossing the two decisions isolates which of them carries the result. Aliasing is worth $+0.157$
and $+0.179$ at the two granularities, keeping the whole block $+0.079$ and $+0.057$ under the two
operations, and the interaction is small and positive. The operation is worth two to three times
the region. That ratio is the practical summary of this work: getting the surviving region right
is a precondition, and choosing the operation is what makes the task possible.

Two questions remain open. The fallback window is the weakest part of the mechanism: when the
answer lies outside it, as in the two-hop variant, recovery depends on the refill order, and
widening the relevant region to cover the answer is the first configuration to test. The
second concerns the privacy claim. A pipeline of this shape would be considerably stronger
with a guarantee that survives an adversary holding the alias map, and establishing one is a
different piece of work from the one reported here.
