\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Prompt compression.}
One family reduces prompt length by scoring and removing tokens or sentences. \textsc{LLMLingua}~\cite{llmlingua} and \textsc{LongLLMLingua}~\cite{longllmlingua}
use a small language model to estimate token informativeness; \textsc{LLMLingua-2}~\cite{llmlingua2}
distils the decision into a token classifier for task-agnostic compression;
Selective Context~\cite{selective_context} drops spans that carry little
self-information; Provence~\cite{provence} prunes sentences jointly with question
relevance; CPC~\cite{cpc} scores sentences with a context-aware encoder;
Quito-X~\cite{quitox} casts compression as an information-bottleneck problem and
approximates mutual information with cross-attention; DAC~\cite{dac} adds an
attention-aware term to an entropy criterion; the Perception
Compressor~\cite{perception_compressor} retrieves relevant demonstrations with guiding
questions before compressing at the token level. These methods differ mainly in how they
estimate relevance and redundancy. They share one assumption: that the prompt is ordinary
text whose only cost is length. None of them alters a value the model is allowed to read,
and none is evaluated on questions whose answer \emph{is} a protected value. The omission is
consequential. The strongest of these methods leaves $98.1\%$ of protected values
recoverable in our benchmark, and it preserves the answer for a query that names a sensitive
entity far less reliably than it preserves an ordinary answer span.

A second design point compresses into continuous space instead of deleting tokens. Gist
tokens~\cite{mu-gist-2023} and in-context autoencoders~\cite{ge-icae-2024} train the reader to
consume a short learned prefix in place of the prompt, AutoCompressors~\cite{chevalier-autocomp-2023}
summarise the context into soft prompt vectors, xRAG~\cite{cheng-xrag-2024} distils a retrieved
passage into a single token, and COCOM~\cite{ren-cocom-2023} inserts sentinel tokens that stand
for a span. Retrieval-side variants compress before the prompt is assembled at
all~\cite{xu-recomp-2024,liu-tcra-2023}, and a parallel line reduces the KV cache rather than the
input~\cite{zhang-h2o-2023,li-snapkv-2024}. Both lines raise the same question for us: what
happens to a value that the question needs to name. Recent work measures how much information
these compressors actually preserve~\cite{lajewska-infopres-2025} and finds the loss is not
uniform across token types, which is consistent with what we report for identifier-dense text.

\paragraph{Text sanitization for inference.}
A second family rewrites protected spans before the prompt leaves the user side.
DYNTEXT~\cite{dyntext} substitutes sensitive tokens with semantically similar non-sensitive
neighbours drawn from a dynamic adjacency list, so that a fixed substitution table cannot be
attacked; INTACT~\cite{intact} replaces identifiers with generalised candidates selected for
resistance to inference attacks; PrivacyMind~\cite{xiao-privacymind-2024} trains a
context-level privacy-preserving reader; PAPILLON~\cite{siyan-papillon-2025} keeps private
spans on a local model and sends only the rest to the cloud; Portcullis~\cite{zhan-portcullis-2025}
and RemoteRAG~\cite{cheng-remoterag-2025} interpose a sanitising gateway between the operator
and a third-party inference service. Masking wrappers around published compressors belong
here too. These methods decide per span what to hide and treat utility as whatever survives
the rewrite. On our benchmark that framing has a measurable cost: a masking pipeline that
achieves zero leakage scores $0.1712$ on the $2{,}728$-case extension, because the span it
removes is the referent the question needs.

\paragraph{Leakage from the inference channel.}
A third body of work asks what a provider can recover from a prompt it receives and retains.
ProPILE~\cite{kim-propile-2023} probes personally identifiable information directly; Staab et
al.~\cite{staab-beyond-2024} show that a reader infers attributes that were never stated;
ReCaLL~\cite{xie-recall-2024} and the lower-ranked-token attack of Zhou et
al.~\cite{zhou-shadows-2025} mount membership inference; Debenedetti et
al.~\cite{debenedetti-sidechannel-2024} catalogue side channels in deployed systems;
Split-and-Denoise~\cite{mai-splitdenoise-2024} perturbs client-side representations under
local differential privacy; and the survey of Das et al.~\cite{das-survey-2025} places these
results in one frame. Our threat model is the narrowest of them: we ask only whether the
protected value is recoverable from the prompt text, and we claim no resistance to attribute
inference or to a provider that manipulates the reader.

\paragraph{Guarantees and prompt-level sanitizers.}
A separate line supplies formal guarantees rather than empirical measurements. Anonymisation
of published microdata is surveyed from $k$-anonymity through $l$-diversity and
$t$-closeness~\cite{carvalho-microdata-2023}; differential privacy is surveyed across the
deployment scenarios in which it is actually applied~\cite{zhao-dp-2024}; and the
prompt-specific end of this line covers privacy engineering for prompts in
general~\cite{edemacu-prompt-2025} and utility-aware prompt sanitisation that, like us,
trades on which span to replace~\cite{shen-prosan-2026}. Each of these bounds what an
adversary can infer from a fixed release. None of them addresses what happens to the answer
when the release must still identify one record per query, which is the behaviour
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to.

\paragraph{IoT logs and their privacy.}
The records we compress are device logs, and the IoT literature protects them at other
layers: probabilistic encoding for aggregate analytics~\cite{zaman-encode-2024},
privacy-preserving data mining with an incentive mechanism~\cite{gao-datamining-2024},
asynchronous federated learning over distributed devices~\cite{yan-fedlearn-2023},
privacy-enhanced role-based access control~\cite{zhang-rbac-2025}, fine-grained cloud-edge
data sharing~\cite{sun-sharing-2025}, and location-aware offloading that keeps raw data at the
edge~\cite{wang-offload-2024}. Log analytics on this data is mature as well, including online
detection under drift~\cite{tian-omlog-2025} and cross-system detection over latent
representations~\cite{zhou-logdlr-2025}, but both consume the log in place. Our unit of
protection is neither the aggregate, the transport, nor the access rule; it is the prompt that
an operator asks about a record, and that unit is what these mechanisms leave uncovered.

\paragraph{Long-context question answering.}
LongBench~\cite{longbench} supplies the passages from which we derive the public-benchmark
variants, and the standard SQuAD~v2 script supplies the scoring function. The evaluation side
of this literature has since moved to harder and longer tasks~\cite{bai-longbenchv2-2025}, to
stress tests that separate genuine long-context ability from retrieval at the right
offset~\cite{hsieh-ruler-2024,yen-helmet-2025}, and to contexts beyond one hundred thousand
tokens~\cite{zhang-infinitebench-2024}. Our contribution is orthogonal to the retrieval and
long-context modelling literature: we do not modify the reader, only the text it receives, and
we measure the effect at a fixed token budget.

\paragraph{Where this study sits.}
The families above optimise different objectives---length, concealment, and the leakage of
everything else a reader can infer---and each reports utility on prompts whose answer is
ordinary text. We study the case where both objectives fall on the same span, and show that
the resulting conflict is resolvable by changing \emph{which operation} acts on that span
rather than by trading one objective against the other. We keep the compression interface of
the first family and the per-span rewriting of the second, and modify the stage at which the
operation is chosen.
